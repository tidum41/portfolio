# AI tells

A checklist for spotting unedited, generated sounding writing and design. Each line is a test: a reviewer can mark it pass or fail on the page in front of them. A tell is a craft problem, not proof of who wrote the page; the fix is always to finish the draft. Sources are named per line and listed in full at the bottom.

- [ ] Buzzword cluster: seamless, robust, elevate, delve, leverage, comprehensive, pivotal, or transformative appears at all, or two approval adjectives (powerful, effortless, intuitive) land in one paragraph. Test: search the page for the list. Example: a 2024 Tübingen and Northwestern study of 14 million PubMed abstracts found "delves" running at 25 times its predicted rate after LLMs arrived.
- [ ] Rule of three padding: benefits, features, or adjectives are grouped in threes more than twice on a page, because the rhythm signals completeness the content has not earned. Test: count the triads. Example: "The Fingerprints of AI Writing" names the rule of three as trimming or padding content into three stages, benefits, or risks.
- [ ] Negative parallelism: "not X, but Y" or "it's not about X, it's about Y" appears as a contrast device. Test: search for "not" constructions that reject a weak frame and replace it with a flattering abstraction. Example: the arXiv detector guide lists "it's not about ___, it's about ___" in its AI vocabulary table.
- [ ] Significance vocabulary doing the work: crucial, key, significant, or pivotal tells the reader a thing matters in place of a sentence showing why. Test: delete the word; if nothing is lost, it was decoration. Example: "The Fingerprints of AI Writing" calls this the significance vocabulary cluster.
- [ ] Vague impact claim: a sentence promises improvement (faster, better, more efficient, more engaging) with no baseline, no mechanism, and no number the writer measured. Test: ask "compared to what, and how do you know" and the page cannot answer. Example: RedHub's craft guide names this confidence with no source, a decision the writer skipped.
- [ ] Symmetric hedging: claims arrive in balanced pairs, a pro matched by an equal con, until the page takes no position anywhere. Test: read only the verdicts; if none exists, the page hedges. Example: RedHub names a hedge where a position belongs as one of the four core tells.
- [ ] Em dash rhythm: em dashes join clauses as the default move, more than twice in a section. Test: count them; this repo's house rule is zero anyway. Note the asymmetry: Graphite's research, reported by Fast Company, found newer models now use em dashes far less than humans, so presence at density is a tell and absence proves nothing.
- [ ] Uniform sentence length: a run of five or more sentences all land at near the same length, with no short sentence doing emphasis work. Test: read aloud; generated drafts rarely stumble, pause, or stop short. Example: RedHub lists sentences that all run the same length as the first mistaken for proof pattern.
- [ ] Transition scaffolding: paragraphs open with Additionally, Moreover, Notably, Ultimately, or "It is important to note". Test: search the paragraph starts. Example: "The Fingerprints of AI Writing" flags these as relations being labeled instead of reasoned, and the arXiv guide lists additionally and moreover as AI adverbs.
- [ ] Abstract landscape words: landscape, ecosystem, tapestry, realm, or journey widens the frame while naming nothing concrete. Test: underline the noun; if you cannot picture or point at it, it is filler. Example: the arXiv detector guide lists landscape, tapestry, and realm among its AI nouns.
- [ ] Decorative gradient: a gradient fills a background, card, or chart area where a flat surface would carry the same meaning. Test: remove it; if nothing becomes harder to read or understand, it was decoration and it fails.
- [ ] Cards for everything, samey spacing: unrelated content sits in identical cards with identical radius, padding, and gap, and one gap value repeats at every level regardless of how related things are. Test: if a rule or whitespace would separate the groups as clearly, the cards are a default, not a decision.
- [ ] Over labeled UI: badges, chips, and category tags announce things the reader can already see (status labels on every figure, a tag on every card). Test: remove a label; if nothing becomes unclear, the label was generated thoroughness, not information.
- [ ] Template symmetry across sections: every section has the same shape, heading, three bullets, summary line, at the same length, with no section allowed to be one line long. Test: compare section outlines; human drafts vary because the material varies.
- [ ] Placeholder facts: specifics that would fit any product ("teams of all sizes", "users love how easy it is") stand where a name, a date, or a measured number belongs. Test: swap in a competitor's name; if the sentence still works, it says nothing. Example: RedHub calls these placeholders where facts belong.
